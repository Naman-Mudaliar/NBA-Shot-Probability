Some shots are easy. Some players make the hard ones anyway.

For every shot of the last two NBA seasons, a model estimated how often an average player would make it, based on where it was taken, what kind of shot it was, the game clock and the defense. It never knows who is shooting. That estimate is the shot's expected make rate (xFG%). Put it next to what actually happened and you can see who makes more than their shots deserve, and who makes less.

02

Who beats the odds?

Add up the expected make rate of every shot a player took and you get how many an average player would have made. The difference from what they actually made is their shot-making. Because the model never sees the shooter, that gap belongs to the player.

Skill, or just a lucky run?

Even an exactly average shooter drifts above or below zero by chance, and the drift grows with more shots. The shaded band is where that luck lands 95% of the time. Dots inside it could be a hot or cold streak; dots outside it almost certainly aren't.

For the technically curious

XGBoost binary classifier on 44 shooter-blind features: shot location (distance, angle, zone, area), shot type (grouped from the play-by-play action type, plus driving/cutting/running flags), game situation (period, time left, home/away), the opponent's make rate allowed in that zone from earlier games only, and the league's recent make rate in that zone. Trained on 2013-14 to 2020-21, early-stopped on 2021-22, then walked forward one week at a time through 2022-23 to 2025-26: predict the week, log it, then continue boosting a few shallow, low-learning-rate trees on a sliding window of recent weeks. "Prediction error" is log loss. The skill-or-luck band is ±2 standard deviations of a sum of independent Bernoulli(xFG) outcomes (× shot value² when counting points); a player's verdict comes from their own z-score. Leakage rules (no shooter inputs, past-only defense, predict-before-train) are enforced by automated tests.
